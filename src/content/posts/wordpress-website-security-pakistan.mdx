WordPress website security in Pakistan is a topic most business owners only think about once. Usually the morning a customer sends a screenshot of their website redirecting to a casino.

Here's the short answer. If your cheap WordPress site is sending visitors to spam, showing strange pages in Google, or has admin users you didn't create, it's probably been hacked. Change every password first, take a backup of the current state, then clean or restore it. And afterwards, fix the reason it happened, which is almost never WordPress itself.

WordPress core is maintained by serious people. The problem is everything bolted on around it, and the fact that after the 15,000 rupee site was handed over, nobody was ever responsible for it again.

## Signs your WordPress site has been hacked

Some are loud. Most are quiet.

- **Redirects to spam.** Visitors land on gambling, pharmacy or "you've won a phone" pages. Often only on mobile, or only when they arrive from Google, so the owner checking on a laptop sees nothing.
- **Strange pages in Google.** Search `site:yourdomain.com`. If you see pages in Japanese, or product listings for things you've never sold, someone has been publishing on your domain.
- **"This site may be hacked" in search results.** Google adds this label when it detects a compromise. Customers see it before they ever reach you.
- **Admin users you didn't create.** Check Users in the dashboard. An extra administrator called something forgettable is a classic.
- **Your host suspends the account.** Often for sending spam email or using too much CPU, because your site is now doing someone else's work.
- **A warning in Search Console.** The [Security Issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101) tells you if Google has flagged hacked content.

If you've never set up Google Search Console, that's the first job after this one. It's free, and it's how Google talks to you.

## Why cheap WordPress sites get hacked

Not bad luck. Usually some combination of:

- **`/wp-admin` with `admin` as the username.** The login sits exactly where every bot expects it, and half the credentials are already guessed.
- **Nulled themes.** Premium themes downloaded free from a forum. Sometimes they come with a gift: a backdoor. (Free premium software is a business model. You're just not the customer.)
- **Thirty plugins, none updated.** Each one is someone else's code running on your site. Outdated ones are how most attacks get in.
- **Nobody responsible.** The developer delivered the site, collected payment and moved on. Updates were never in the deal. Nobody told you that.

## What to do first if you've been hacked

Calmly, in this order:

1. **Change every password.** Hosting control panel, WordPress, database, email, FTP. All of them, from a clean device.
2. **Take a full backup of the current state.** Yes, the hacked one. It's evidence, and you may need it to find how they got in.
3. **Call your host.** Many have malware scanning and can tell you what changed and when.
4. **Clean or restore.** Restore from a backup you know is clean, or have someone clean the infection properly. Deleting the one file you noticed isn't cleaning. Hacks usually leave more than one door open.
5. **Close the door they used.** Update everything, delete unused plugins and themes, replace any nulled theme, remove unknown users.
6. **Tell Google.** Once it's clean, request a review in Search Console so the warnings come off.

## WordPress website security: how to stop it happening again

The [WordPress hardening guide](https://developer.wordpress.org/advanced-administration/security/hardening/) is genuinely good. The short version:

- **Update WordPress, themes and plugins** regularly. Set a monthly reminder if nothing else.
- **Delete what you don't use.** A deactivated plugin is still code on your server.
- **No nulled anything.** Ever.
- **A real username, a strong password and two-factor login** for every admin.
- **Off-site backups,** tested at least once, because a backup you've never restored is a rumour.
- **One named person responsible.** In writing. If that's your developer, it's part of the deal, not a favour.

## When to stop patching and move on

Honest tradeoff: WordPress is fine. Properly built, updated and maintained, it runs a huge share of the web safely. If your site is small and you're willing to look after it, harden it and keep it.

If it's been hacked twice, nobody wants to own the updates, and the site mostly exists to show buyers who you are, a static site might suit you better. No public login, no plugins, no database behind every page, so far less to attack. We compare both in [WordPress vs Next.js for a business website](/blog/wordpress-vs-nextjs-for-business-website).
