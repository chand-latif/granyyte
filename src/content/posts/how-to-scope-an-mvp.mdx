MVP scope is the part of building an app that everyone agrees matters and nobody wants to do. It's homework. It has no screens. Nobody claps for a scope document.

And yet it decides almost everything else: the timeline, the price, whether the agent-assisted build takes one week or seven, and whether version one ships at all or slowly turns into version one of everything.

Here's the short answer. A good MVP scope is one core job, the single flow that delivers it, and a written list of everything you're not building yet. That last list is the one that saves you.

## Why MVP scope matters more than ever

Building got fast. With AI coding agents, a senior team ships a focused MVP in 1 to 3 weeks. We explain why in [how long it takes to build an app](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-an-app).

The catch: agents build what the scope says. A clear scope gets built clearly, at speed. A vague scope gets built vaguely, also at speed, and then rebuilt. Scope used to be a document the developer skimmed. Now it's close to the actual instructions. We wrote more on that in [AI coding agents won't save a project nobody understands](/blog/ai-coding-agents-software-development).

## The MVP scope in five questions

Answer these in writing. Short answers are fine. Vague ones aren't.

### 1. What's the one job?

Not the vision. The job. "Let dealers request a gate pass without phoning anyone" is a job. "Revolutionise the auction industry" is a LinkedIn bio.

[AVC Dealer](/work/avc-dealer), a dealer app across 50+ auction locations in 25 US states, replaced phone calls (and hold music) for a list of very concrete jobs. Concrete jobs scope well. Visions don't.

### 2. Who's using it?

List the user roles. Customer, admin, maybe a staff member. Every extra role multiplies screens, permissions and testing. Three roles is a real app. Six roles is a platform, and platforms aren't MVPs.

### 3. What's the core flow?

Walk through it step by step, from opening the app to getting the result. Every screen on that path is in. Most screens off that path are out.

### 4. What has to plug into something else?

Payments, maps, a WhatsApp message, an existing system. Integrations are where timelines hide, so name every one. "And it syncs with our accounting software" is one line in a brief and a whole week in a build.

### 5. What are we not building?

The most important section, and the one people skip. Write it down. Social features, dark mode, the referral programme, the AI assistant, the second language, the web version. Not rejected. Just not now.

## A one-page MVP scope template

Copy this, fill it in, and send it to any developer you're talking to. Including us.

- **The problem:** one or two sentences.
- **Who it's for:** the user roles, and roughly how many users at launch.
- **The core job:** one sentence.
- **The core flow:** numbered steps, from opening the app to the result.
- **Screens:** a list, one line each.
- **Integrations:** payments, maps, messaging, existing systems.
- **Admin side:** what someone on your team needs to see or change.
- **Not included in version one:** the list. Be generous.
- **How we'll know it worked:** one number you'll check after a month.
- **Decision-maker:** one name.

If you can fill that in, a good team can give you a fixed price and a timeline from it. That's not a coincidence. It's the same information they need. We explain why fixed price works best for an MVP in [fixed price vs hourly app development](/blog/fixed-price-vs-hourly-app-development).

## How to cut without crying

Everyone's first draft is too big. That's fine. Cut with three questions:

- **Can a user still get the core result without it?** Then it moves to version two.
- **Can a human do it by hand for now?** Approving accounts, sending a weekly report, onboarding the first ten customers. Automate it when it's annoying, not before.
- **Did a real user ask for it, or did someone in a meeting?** Meetings generate a lot of features. Users generate the useful ones.

Then cut once more. The feature you're most attached to is often the one that can wait.

## The red flags in your own scope

- "And also..." appears more than three times.
- More than one person thinks they're the decision-maker.
- The not-included list is empty.
- The app has to be "like Uber, but for", and the second half is still being decided.
- Someone wants an AI chatbot and can't say what it's for. (We covered when a chatbot earns its place in [adding an AI chat assistant to a mobile app](/blog/ai-chatbot-in-mobile-app).)

## The honest tradeoff

A tight scope means version one will feel small. Some things you care about won't be in it, and that'll be uncomfortable for a few weeks.

That's the deal. A small app that works and ships in weeks teaches you more than a large app that's "nearly ready" for six months. Apple's own guidelines expect an app to offer [real functionality](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#minimum-functionality), so small doesn't mean hollow. It means one job done properly, with everything else waiting its turn on the version two list. Which, if you've done this right, is already longer than version one.
