You've decided to hire offshore developers. Congratulations. You're about to save a lot of money, or spend a lot of money twice. There's rarely a third outcome.

The difference isn't the country. It isn't the hourly rate. It's about six questions, asked before anybody signs anything, that most people skip because the proposal looked nice and the developer said "no problem, sir" eleven times on the first call.

"No problem" is not a delivery plan.

We're an offshore team ourselves, in Sialkot, Pakistan, so yes, we'd like you to hire offshore developers. Ideally us. But we'd rather you hire anyone using these questions than hire us without them, because the people who skip them are the ones who end up paying for a rebuild.

## Question 1: "Can I download something you built?"

Not see a screenshot. Not watch a Loom. Download it.

A portfolio full of Dribbble shots tells you somebody can use Figma. A live app on the App Store tells you somebody got through Apple's review, handled payments or logins, and shipped. It's the closest thing to proof this industry has.

So ask for store links, then go and read the listings. Check the release history. Check the reviews. We point people at [Bilge AI](/work/bilge-ai), which has more than 15,000 users in Turkey, and [AVC Dealer](/work/avc-dealer), a dealer app covering 50+ auction locations across 25 US states. Not because they're the only things we've built. Because anybody can verify them in thirty seconds.

If the answer is "our work is under NDA, all of it", that's either a very unlucky team or a very convenient sentence.

## Question 2: "Who is actually writing the code?"

This is the subcontracting question, and it's the one that causes the most quiet damage.

You hire a confident salesperson with a nice website. They pass your project to a cheaper developer, who passes half of it to a cheaper one still. Nobody lies, exactly. Nobody tells you either. You find out in month three, when the person on your calls can't answer a technical question without "let me check with the team".

Ask for the name of the person writing your code, and talk to them before you sign. If that conversation keeps not happening, you have your answer.

## Question 3: "Where does the code live, and who owns it?"

The right answer is: in a repository you own, from the first week, with you as an admin.

The wrong answers include "we'll send you the code at the end", "it's on our server", and the classic, a long pause.

If you only receive the code after final payment, you're trusting the relationship to survive every disagreement between now and then. Some won't. We wrote a whole post on [what owning your source code actually means](/blog/source-code-ownership-software-development), because "you own the code" is said far more often than it's true.

## Question 4: "What happens when we disagree about scope?"

You will. Every project does. The question is whether there's a written answer for it.

A good offshore team writes a scope before quoting, prices against it, and has a simple change process: new idea, written estimate, you approve or don't. A bad one quotes from a ten-minute call and then discovers, mid-project, that everything you assumed was included is "a new feature".

This is also where pricing models matter. We explain the tradeoffs in [fixed price vs hourly for app development](/blog/fixed-price-vs-hourly-app-development), but the short version is: a vague scope on an hourly contract is a blank cheque with a time zone.

## Question 5: "How will we talk, and how often?"

Time zones aren't the problem people think they are. Silence is.

Agree on the basics upfront: a weekly call, a shared channel, a working build you can install every week or two, and who answers when something breaks. A team that sends you something to tap on regularly is a team that's actually building. A team that sends weekly "progress update: progressing well" emails is a team you should phone.

The time difference can even help. For a client in the US, feedback sent at the end of their day can turn into changes waiting for them in the morning. Overnight progress feels like magic. It's mostly just geography. And for the Gulf, where we built [Zwipe](/work/zwipe), a car marketplace in the UAE, Pakistan is barely an hour apart, so there isn't much time difference to manage at all.

## Question 6: "What happens after launch?"

Launch is not the end. It's the moment real users find all the things testers didn't.

Ask what the first month after launch looks like. Who fixes bugs, how fast, and at what cost? What happens when Apple or Google changes a rule and your app needs an update to stay in the store? (They do this. Regularly. Without asking.)

A team that goes quiet after the final invoice wasn't a partner. They were a vending machine.

## The red flags, in one list

- No live, downloadable work.
- Nobody technical on the sales calls.
- A price given before anyone asked you a real question.
- Code that "will be shared at the end".
- Daily enthusiasm, weekly silence.

If you see two of these, keep looking. If you see four, they found your number on a list.

## Where offshore genuinely isn't right

Honest tradeoff: if your product needs someone in the room every day, say a hardware startup iterating on physical prototypes, or a team that changes direction three times a week by shouting across a desk, a local team will probably serve you better, even at a higher cost.

And if your budget only covers the cheapest bid on a freelance marketplace, offshore or not, you're not hiring a team. You're buying a lottery ticket with a deadline.

## How to hire offshore developers, the short version

Hire offshore developers the way you'd hire anyone you'll trust with something expensive. Check their work, meet the people, own the code, write things down, and agree what happens after launch.

Six questions. About an hour. Considerably cheaper than a rebuild.
