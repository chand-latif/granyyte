Custom CRM vs HubSpot is a decision most teams make by vibes. Someone sees a demo, someone else sees an invoice, and the loudest person in the meeting wins. Let's use arithmetic instead. It's less exciting and considerably more accurate.

Here's the short answer. If HubSpot fits your process, use HubSpot. If your team is growing, needs paid tiers and works in a way HubSpot keeps fighting, a custom CRM usually wins over three years, and sometimes sooner.

We build custom CRMs and operations systems, so yes, we have a horse in this race. Which is why the first section is about when not to hire us.

## When HubSpot is the right answer

HubSpot is a very good product. If your sales process is leads, deals, emails, a pipeline and a weekly report, it does all of that well, on day one, with no build at all.

Use it when:

- **Your process is standard.** If HubSpot fits about 90% of how you work, the last 10% isn't worth a build.
- **You're small and staying small.** A few users on the free or starter tiers is hard to beat on cost.
- **Marketing is the main event.** Email campaigns, landing pages, forms and lead scoring are where HubSpot really shines.

We say the same thing in our wider guide to [custom software vs off-the-shelf](/blog/custom-software-vs-off-the-shelf): buy first, build when buying stops working.

## Custom CRM vs HubSpot: the per-seat sum

Now the arithmetic. At the time of writing (November 2026), [HubSpot's pricing page](https://www.hubspot.com/pricing/sales) lists Sales Hub Professional at around $90 per seat per month on annual billing, plus a one-time onboarding fee. These are HubSpot's public prices, not ours, and they change, so check them yourself.

For a 10-person sales and ops team on that tier:

- **Per month:** 10 seats × $90 = $900.
- **Per year:** $10,800.
- **Over three years:** $32,400, plus onboarding.

Now hire five more people. The bill grows by half. Hire ten more and it doubles. Per-seat pricing is a tax on growth, and it's very politely worded.

Add the extras teams usually need sooner or later, like higher tiers for specific features or add-ons for other departments, and the three-year number tends to go up, not down.

A custom CRM works the other way around. You pay to build it, then you pay for hosting and maintenance. Adding the eleventh user costs roughly nothing. Adding the fiftieth costs roughly nothing too.

## What you get for the money

The arithmetic only matters if the custom version is actually better for you. It usually is when:

- **Your process isn't a sales pipeline.** Jobs, site visits, approvals, inspections, orders moving through production. HubSpot can be bent into these shapes. Bent things snap.
- **Rules need enforcing, not suggesting.** "Don't move to the next step until this document is uploaded" is a custom field and a hope in most CRMs. In your own system it's a rule.
- **Everyone needs access.** Field staff, contractors, part-timers. On per-seat pricing, you start deciding who's important enough to get a login. That's a strange way to run a company.

The clearest example from our own work: the [operations platform we built for Space Maintenance](/work/surveyor-job-management-system), a surveyor-led firm in the UK. It runs jobs from creation to sign-off, sends contractors job links on WhatsApp and email, and enforces "No RAMS, no work" in the software. It runs on Supabase with no per-seat pricing, so the firm grows its team and job volume without the software bill growing with it. It also sits alongside the off-the-shelf tool they already used, which brings us to the middle path.

## The middle path: HubSpot plus a custom tool

You don't always have to choose. Plenty of teams keep HubSpot for marketing and email, and build a custom tool for the one part of the business HubSpot handles badly. HubSpot has a solid API, so the two can share contacts and deals.

It's less dramatic than "we replaced HubSpot". It's often the cheaper, faster and more sensible answer.

## How long a custom CRM takes

With AI coding agents doing much of the typing, a focused CRM for one team takes 1 to 3 weeks. A fuller one with several roles, automations and integrations takes 2 to 4 weeks. Larger multi-department systems take 3 to 6 weeks, built in phases so people start using the first parts early. We explain where the time goes in [how long it takes to build an app](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-an-app).

And whoever builds it, the code and database should live in your accounts from day one. We wrote a whole post on [what owning your source code actually means](/blog/source-code-ownership-software-development).

## The honest bottom line

If you're five people with a standard pipeline, close this tab and sign up for HubSpot. Really.

If you're ten people heading for twenty, your process looks nothing like the demo, and half your team doesn't have a login because seats cost money, do the three-year sum. Then do it again with the team you'll have next year. The answer usually becomes obvious, and it's rarely "keep paying more to fit in less".
