Fixed price vs hourly for app development sounds like a pricing question. It isn't. It's a question about who gets to be nervous.

On a fixed price, the developer is nervous. If the work takes longer than they estimated, that's their problem.

On hourly, you're nervous. Every week. Usually on Friday, when the timesheet arrives.

Neither model is a scam. Both can go badly. Here's how each one works, how each one goes wrong, and which one fits what you're building.

## Fixed price: you buy an outcome

You agree on a written scope, the developer quotes one price for it, and you pay in milestones as working pieces are delivered.

What's good about it:

- **You know the number before you start.** Your budget conversation happens once, not every month.
- **The hard decisions happen upfront.** To quote a fixed price, a team has to understand the app properly. That forces questions you'd otherwise discover in month three.
- **The developer carries the risk.** If their estimate was optimistic, they eat it.

How it goes wrong:

- **A vague scope.** "Uber but for dog walkers" is not a scope. A fixed price on a vague scope just moves the argument to the end of the project.
- **The change-request trap.** Some teams quote low, then treat everything not literally written down as an extra. Your app becomes a menu with surprise prices.
- **Corner-cutting.** If a team quoted too low, the temptation is to finish fast rather than well. Testing is usually the first thing quietly dropped.

## Hourly: you buy time

You pay for the hours worked, usually invoiced weekly or monthly. Sometimes it's called "time and materials", which sounds more serious and costs the same.

What's good about it:

- **Total flexibility.** Change direction on Tuesday, and on Wednesday the team is building the new thing. No renegotiation.
- **Fits genuine uncertainty.** Research, prototypes, ongoing improvements to a live product, and anything where you can't describe "done" yet.
- **No risk premium.** You're not paying for the developer's buffer against a bad estimate.

How it goes wrong:

- **The open tap.** Without a scope, hours have no natural stopping point. The project ends when your money does.
- **Incentives pointing the wrong way.** Nobody sets out to work slowly. But when slower is paid more, nothing pushes the other way.
- **Hours you can't verify.** "Research, 6 hours" on a timesheet could be research. It could also be lunch.

The honest summary: a vague scope on an hourly contract is a blank cheque with a time zone.

## Fixed price vs hourly: which fits what you're building?

**Choose fixed price when:**

- It's a first version or an MVP with a clear, written scope.
- You have a set budget and need to know the total.
- You're hiring a team you haven't worked with before. A fixed price is a much smaller leap of faith.

**Choose hourly when:**

- The product is live and you want steady, ongoing improvements.
- You genuinely don't know what you're building yet and need to explore.
- You have someone technical on your side who can read the hours and push back.

**The hybrid most people miss:** fixed price for version one, then a monthly arrangement for ongoing work once you trust each other. You get a known cost while risk is highest, and flexibility once the product is real.

We quote every project as a fixed price against a written scope, usually within 1 to 2 days of the first conversation. That's partly about trust and partly about discipline. Writing a proper scope makes us ask the awkward questions early, while they're still cheap to answer.

It also means we carry the risk of our own estimates, which keeps us honest about them. The tradeoff is real: if your product changes direction every week, fixed price will feel rigid, and an hourly team with good reporting may suit you better. We'll say so if that's you.

## What to put in the contract, either way

- **A written scope:** screens, features, user roles, integrations.
- **What's not included.** This list prevents more arguments than the scope itself.
- **Milestones tied to working software,** not to "phase 2 complete".
- **A change process:** written request, written estimate, your approval, then work.
- **Code ownership:** the code lives in your repository from day one. We wrote a whole post on [what owning your source code actually means](/blog/source-code-ownership-software-development).
- **After launch:** who fixes bugs, how fast, and on what terms.

## The bottom line

Fixed price buys certainty and makes the developer nervous. Hourly buys flexibility and makes you nervous. Pick whichever matches how well you know what you want.

Just make sure someone is nervous. Projects where nobody is are the ones that never finish.
