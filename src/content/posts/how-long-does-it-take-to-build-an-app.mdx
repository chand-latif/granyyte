How long does it take to build an app? Somewhere between "one week" and "we're still in discovery", depending entirely on who you ask and how badly they want the contract.

You've heard both. The agency that quotes nine months and a discovery phase that costs more than your car. The freelancer who says "one week, very easy, no problem", which is technically a timeline in the same way "soon" is technically a date.

Here's the honest version, from people who ship apps for a living and would quite like you to believe us next time too.

## How long does it take to build an app? The short answer

- **A focused MVP:** 1 to 3 weeks, depending on the requirements. One core flow, logins, a basic admin side, published to the App Store and Google Play.
- **A full product:** 2 to 4 weeks. Several user roles, payments, notifications, an admin dashboard, a couple of integrations.
- **Large software:** 3 to 6 weeks. Multiple apps or portals, complex workflows, heavier integrations, and a proper rollout plan.

If those numbers look suspiciously short, good. A couple of years ago they would have been. The agency still quoting nine months isn't being careful. It's billing you for how software used to be built.

## Why apps take weeks now, not months

Two things changed.

**One codebase.** We build with Flutter and FlutterFlow, so iOS and Android come out of the same project at the same time. You don't pay for the app twice. We explain the tradeoffs in [FlutterFlow vs native development](/blog/flutterflow-vs-native-development).

**AI coding agents.** This is the big one. A senior engineer directing a good coding agent now writes in an afternoon what used to take a week: screens, data models, tests, all the boring plumbing nobody misses writing by hand. The whole industry got faster, and anyone still pricing by the old clock is either behind or hoping you are.

The catch, and it matters: an agent is only as good as the engineer's understanding of the whole system. It fills every gap in its context with an assumption. Hand it to someone who doesn't understand the product and it will confidently build the wrong thing, very quickly. Speed without understanding just gets you to the rebuild sooner.

So the time didn't disappear. It moved. Less typing, more thinking: scoping, architecture, and checking what the agent wrote.

## Where the time actually goes

People imagine an app timeline as "coding". With agents doing most of the typing, coding is now the fast part. The rest is decisions, and decisions are slow.

### Scoping (a few days)

Writing down what the app does, screen by screen, and what it doesn't do. This is the most valuable couple of days you'll spend, and the one everyone wants to skip. It's also what the agents build from, so a vague scope gets built vaguely, at speed.

Skip it, and you'll do it anyway. Just later, mid-build, at a higher price, and with a lot more sighing.

### Design (days, not months)

If you already have designs or a clear reference ("like this app, but for this"), design is quick. If you're designing from nothing and everyone on your team has opinions about the shade of blue, budget accordingly. The shade of blue has delayed more launches than any bug.

### Building (the bulk)

Screens, logic, the database, logins, the admin side. With a clear scope and a good agent, this moves faster than ever. With a moving scope, it moves in circles. Quicker circles, but circles.

### Testing and fixes (always longer than you'd like)

Real devices, real data, real edge cases. What happens when someone's phone is in Turkish? When the network drops mid-payment? When a user types an emoji where their phone number should go? (They will.)

### App store review (usually short, occasionally not)

Apple says most submissions are reviewed [within 24 hours](https://developer.apple.com/distribute/app-review/). Google Play is often quick too. But a first submission can take longer, and a rejection sends you back to the start of the queue. Never announce a launch date that has no buffer for this. Apple doesn't read your press release.

## The five things that turn 3 weeks into 3 months

None of these are technical, and no coding agent fixes them. That's the annoying part.

1. **Scope creep.** "Small addition" is the most expensive phrase in software. Five small additions are a second project.
2. **Slow feedback.** If a question waits four days for an answer, the timeline grows by four days. On a three-week build, that's a fifth of the project spent waiting for a reply.
3. **Missing content.** The app is ready. The copy, the images, the terms and the privacy policy are "coming this week". They've been coming this week for a month.
4. **Late decisions.** Which payment provider? Do we need a login at all? Changing these after the build has started means rebuilding the bits that depend on them.
5. **The committee.** Six people who each need to approve every screen, none of whom are in the same meeting. Pick one decision-maker. Your timeline will thank you.

In our experience, number 2 costs more days than every bug combined.

## How to make it faster (without cutting corners)

- **Cut version one down hard.** One core job, done well. Everything else goes on a list for version two. [Bilge AI](/work/bilge-ai), now past 15,000 users, is built around one clear job: the big AI models behind one Turkish-first interface. One job is easier to ship than twelve.
- **Have one person who can say yes.** Same day, ideally.
- **Bring references.** Three apps you like and why beats a forty-page requirements document nobody finishes reading.
- **Agree a fixed scope and price.** It forces the hard decisions to the start, where they're cheap.
- **Get a working build early.** Tapping a real app in the first week surfaces problems no document ever will.

## When a fast timeline is a red flag

Honest tradeoff time. If somebody quotes a complex product, with payments, three user roles, real-time chat and an admin dashboard, in three days, they're not fast. They're either reusing a template they haven't mentioned, or they're about to let an agent guess its way through your brief.

Speed comes from a tight scope, a team that's done it before, and an engineer who actually reads what the agent wrote. Not from optimism.

And if your app genuinely needs heavy device-level work (Bluetooth hardware, AR, long background processing), expect longer, whoever builds it. Physics doesn't do discounts.

## So, how long will yours take?

It depends on the scope, which is why any honest answer starts with questions, not a number. Tell a team what the app needs to do, who uses it and what "done" looks like for version one, and a good one will give you a timeline in writing, with the assumptions listed.

If they can't list the assumptions, the timeline is a guess wearing a tie.
