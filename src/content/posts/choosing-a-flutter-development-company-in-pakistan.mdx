## Why Pakistan is good at Flutter in the first place

Flutter lets one codebase run natively on iOS and Android, which roughly halves the work of building an app twice. Pakistan has a large, young developer community that adopted it early, plenty of English-speaking engineers, and a working day that overlaps European mornings and US evenings.

The flip side is range. The market goes from students selling five-dollar gigs to agencies quoting like they rent an office in Canary Wharf. Both ends will happily tell you they're the best. Your job is to find out who's actually shipped.

## Seven checks that sort the market in an afternoon

### 1. Live apps on both stores, right now

Not screenshots. Not a Dribbble shot. Not "it's under NDA" five times in a row. Ask for App Store and Google Play links to Flutter apps they built, then install one while you're on the call.

For the record, ours include [Bilge AI](/work/bilge-ai), a Turkish-first app with more than 15,000 users that puts GPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok behind one interface, [Zwipe](/work/zwipe), a car marketplace in the UAE with real-time chat, and [Poland Portal](/work/poland-portal) for expats in Poland. You can download all three today, which is the whole point.

### 2. Who writes the code?

Ask to meet the engineer, not the account manager. A surprising number of "companies" are a confident salesperson in front of a rotating cast of freelancers. That can work, but you should know about it before the deposit, not after the third missed deadline.

### 3. Do you own the source code?

You should get the full source code, the store accounts in your name, and access to the backend. A vague answer here ("we'll host it for you, don't worry") is the single best reason to walk away. The most expensive app is the one you have to rebuild because someone else holds the keys.

### 4. Fixed price against a written scope

Hourly billing on a loose brief is a blank cheque with a progress bar. A good Flutter development company will write down what's being built, price it as a fixed amount, and requote openly if the scope changes. If they can't write the scope, they don't understand the project yet.

### 5. Weekly builds you can tap through

You shouldn't wait until week six to see something. Ask for a test build on your own phone every week (TestFlight on iPhone, an internal test track on Android). It keeps everyone honest, including the people who'd rather show you a nice Figma file.

### 6. Store publishing experience

Getting an app through Apple's review is its own small sport. Ask how many apps they've personally submitted, what got rejected, and what they did about it. Anyone who says "never had a rejection" either hasn't shipped much or is being creative with the truth.

### 7. Will they tell you when Flutter is wrong?

This is our favourite test. Ask, "When would you not use Flutter for this?" A good answer mentions things like heavy device-level work, AR, or a team that already has native developers. A bad answer is "Flutter can do everything." It can do most things. That's different.

## Red flags worth walking away from

- A portfolio full of concepts and no store links.
- Prices that are suspiciously round and suspiciously low, with no written scope. The real number turns up later.
- No contract, or a contract with nothing about code ownership.
- Every question answered by a salesperson who has to "check with the team".
- Pressure to start this week before you've seen a scope.

None of these mean the people are bad. They mean you can't verify them, and that's what costs money.

## Freelancer, agency, or something in between?

Freelancers in Pakistan can be excellent, and for a small, tightly scoped app a strong one is hard to beat on value. The risks are the usual ones: a single point of failure, and nobody to pick up the project if life happens.

Big agencies bring process, but you'll often be handed to a junior team while the senior people pitch the next client.

The option we've built Granyyte around sits in the middle: a lean, senior-led team where you talk directly to the people writing your code, with agency-style contracts, store publishing and full handover. We would say that, obviously. Use the seven checks anyway.

## What a good proposal looks like

When you get a proposal back, it should fit on a few pages and answer these without you asking:

| Section | What it should say |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Screens and features, written plainly, including what's *not* included |
| Price | One fixed figure, with milestones tied to working builds |
| Timeline | Real dates, and what they depend on |
| Tech | Flutter or FlutterFlow, the backend (Firebase, Supabase or custom), and why |
| Handover | Source code, store accounts, backend access, documentation |
| After launch | Who fixes bugs, for how long, and how support is priced |

If a proposal is mostly adjectives, it's a brochure.
