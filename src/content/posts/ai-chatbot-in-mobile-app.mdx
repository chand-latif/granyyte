A lot of app briefs now have the same line somewhere near the bottom: "and an AI chatbot".

Sometimes it's the best feature in the app. Sometimes it's a very expensive way to say "hello, how can I help you today?" to people who didn't ask. This post is about telling those two apart, and about what adding an **AI chatbot in a mobile app** actually involves once you get past the demo.

We've shipped two apps with AI at the centre. [Bilge AI](/work/bilge-ai) puts GPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok behind one Turkish-first interface and has more than 15,000 users. [SCAMalicious](/work/scamalicious), an anti-scam app with more than 5,000 downloads on Google Play, has an assistant powered by OpenAI and Grok models that answers questions about suspicious situations in plain language. So this isn't theory, it's what we think about on every AI build.

## Step one: the app should never hold the keys

The quickest way to add a chatbot is to paste an API key into the app and call the AI provider directly. It works in the demo. It also hands your key to anyone patient enough to unpack the app, and a leaked key means a stranger can run up your bill at your expense.

So the first rule: **the app talks to your backend, and your backend talks to the AI.** In SCAMalicious, the backend side is Firebase Cloud Functions plus custom APIs for the AI models. In other projects it might be a small server of your own. Either way, the middle layer is where the real work happens:

- The API keys live there, out of reach.
- You decide which model answers which kind of question.
- You add limits per user, so one enthusiastic user can't spend your month's budget in an afternoon.
- You can switch models or providers without waiting for app store review.

That last point matters more than it sounds. AI models change monthly. App updates take days. Keep the decision on the server.

## Step two: pick models for the job, not the headline

There's no single best model. There's the one that answers *your* users' questions well, fast enough, at a cost that still works when you have ten thousand of them.

Bilge AI took this to its logical end and offers four providers, because its whole point is putting every major model in one place. Most apps don't need that. But using more than one provider is worth considering for two practical reasons: you can fall back when one is slow or down, and you can send simple questions to a cheaper model and harder ones to a stronger model.

We'd also say this plainly: test with real questions from real users, not the examples in the provider's launch post. Your users will ask things nobody in a marketing team imagined.

## Step three: give it a job and some rules

A general chatbot dropped into a specialist app is a liability. Ask a scam-awareness app's assistant about cooking and it shouldn't cheerfully help with lasagne.

The fix is a clear system prompt and some guardrails:

- **Stay on topic.** The assistant knows what the app is for and politely declines everything else.
- **Know its limits.** In a safety app, the assistant should help users think a situation through and point them to proper reporting channels, not pretend to be the police.
- **Be honest about uncertainty.** "This has several warning signs of a scam" is helpful. "This is 100% a scam" is a promise no model can keep.

This is where most of the actual design work goes. The UI of a chat screen takes a day. Deciding what the assistant should and shouldn't say takes considerably longer, and it's the part users notice.

## Step four: plan for the bill

AI providers charge for every bit of text that goes in and comes out. More users, longer chats, longer prompts: all of it costs money, every month.

A few habits keep it under control:

- Keep the system prompt tight. It's sent with every message.
- Trim old conversation history instead of resending the whole thing each time.
- Set sensible daily limits per user.
- Tie heavier use to a paid tier.

On that last point, SCAMalicious uses [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/docs) to handle subscriptions on both the App Store and Google Play. It saves you from building two separate billing systems and arguing with two separate stores about receipts. If the AI feature is expensive to run, it's perfectly reasonable for it to be part of what people pay for.

## When a chatbot is a gimmick

Here's the honest part. Plenty of apps don't need one.

A chatbot earns its place when users have follow-up questions the app can't predict: "is this message from my bank real?", "what does this contract clause mean?", "which model writes better Turkish?". It doesn't earn its place when the app already has three buttons that do everything a user needs, and the chatbot just makes them type instead of tap.

If you're building an MVP, ask: would the app still be useful without the assistant? If yes, ship without it, see what users actually ask for in your reviews and support messages, and add AI where those questions cluster. That's cheaper, and the assistant you build later will be far better aimed.
